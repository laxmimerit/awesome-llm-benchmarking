Architecture

There is no new architecture in Ornith 1.5

The model cards describe an "end-to-end self-improvement loop" and never publish a layer count. The GGUF headers settle it: the 9B declares general.architecture = qwen35 and the 35B declares qwen35moe. Both are Qwen 3.5 with different weights. Whatever these models are worth is entirely post-training, which is why the quality and agentic sections below carry the verdict rather than the speed sections.

One thing the cards omit entirely: both Ornith GGUFs ship a working multi-token-prediction head, a nextn block that llama.cpp ignores unless you pass --spec-type draft-mtp. Nobody reading the documentation would know it was there.

The 35B is an unusually granular mixture: 256 experts of width 512 hanging off a residual stream only 2,048 wide, with 8 experts plus a shared one active per token. That is roughly 3B parameters touched per forward pass, and it is the whole reason the speed numbers below look the way they do.

Worth noting for anyone comparing against the card: Gemma 4-31B is dense. The gemma4:26b sitting in most Ollama stores is a different model, a 128-expert MoE. Same family name, different architecture.

Muse-Glimmer-30B15,968 MiB52, 13 full + 39 windowed13.0 KiB3.2 GiB302 MiB

Every qwen35 model holds KV on only a quarter of its blocks, and the reason is not cache sharing: it is a hybrid stack. A real attention block sits at every fourth position (indices 3, 7, 11 and so on) and the three blocks between each pair are Gated DeltaNet, carrying ssm_alpha, ssm_beta and ssm_out instead of key and value projections. Those blocks keep a fixed-size recurrent state that does not grow with context, so they never allocate a cache at all. Ornith 9B is 8 attention blocks of 32, the 35B is 10 of 40, Qwen 3.8 is 16 of 64. The 35B is the most frugal of the three at 20 KiB per token.

Gemma and Muse both use sliding-window attention and both allocate two caches, but they land at opposite extremes. Muse's 39 windowed layers cost 97.5 MiB total regardless of context. Gemma's ten full-attention layers are so wide, head dimension 512, that it ends up the most expensive model per token in the field despite the windowing. That single fact drives everything that goes wrong for Gemma below.

KV quantization behaves identically across all five: 47% smaller at q8_0, 72% at q4_0. It is a uniform lever, not a model-specific one. The quality cost of that compression was not measured here.

Context ceiling

Every advertised context length is a fiction on a 32 GB card

All five models advertise 262,144 tokens. All five will start at 262,144 and report it cleanly in /props. That number means nothing, and finding out why was the most interesting result in this benchmark.

The mechanism

On Windows, a CUDA allocation that exceeds VRAM does not fail. The driver backs it with system RAM over PCIe. Gemma's own numbers show it happening: from context 155,648 to 262,144 its KV cache grew by 8.3 GiB while measured VRAM never moved off 31.5 GiB. That extra 8 GiB was living in host memory, and nothing in any log said so.

So "does it load" has no failure point on this platform and is the wrong question. The right one is where the performance cliff sits. Below, each model's ceiling is the largest context at which it still decodes within 90% of its own short-context rate.

This is the practical headline of the whole benchmark. Ornith 35B-A3B is the only large model here that delivers its full advertised context at full speed, and it does so with almost 6 GiB of headroom to spare, because 20 KiB per token over 262k tokens is only 5.0 GiB of KV. Gemma needs 20.0 GiB for the same window and cannot have it.

Long context in use

Retrieval is perfect everywhere; the cost is not

Needle retrieval at 16k, 64k and 128k, with the needle at 10% and 90% depth: every model found every needle, 6/6, at every depth. Accuracy is not the differentiator. Time is.

Gemma takes three and a half minutes to answer what Ornith answers in 26 seconds. It was running at 139,264 context in that phase, just past its cliff, so part of that penalty is the spill described above. Muse is the quiet winner on shape: sliding-window attention means its prefill decays only 20% from 16k to 128k, against 46% for Qwen.

Acceptance rate is the whole story: around 0.63 to 0.86 on structured output where the next tokens are predictable, falling to about 0.30 on prose, at which point the drafting overhead costs more than it saves. Qwen 3.8's head is markedly better trained than either Ornith head.

Not a free speedup

Two greedy runs of the same model with MTP off are bit-identical. With MTP on, the output diverges — on 14 of 15 model and content combinations tested. Disabling backend sampling changed nothing (acceptance rates stayed identical to three decimal places). So MTP on this build is a different configuration producing different text, not a transparent accelerator, and its speed numbers should never be folded into a headline tok/s figure. Whether the different text is worse was not measured.

Thirty-nine of forty correct. The single miss was Qwen 3.8 on one hard problem. Nothing truncated, so no model ran away with its thinking budget.

Two findings hide in the columns. Token count and wall clock point in opposite directions. Qwen 3.8 is the most token-economical model in the field at 1,420 median tokens, and it still takes 2.4× longer than Ornith 35B to produce an answer, purely because it decodes at 77 tok/s instead of 272. Quoting either number alone would mislead.

And the Ornith models think proportionally less: 70% of output inside the think block for the 35B, against 95% for Muse and 89% for Qwen. Muse spends almost its entire budget deliberating. Ornith commits sooner.

The 9B is the interesting counter-case: it reaches the same 8/8 by thinking harder, 3,481 tokens against its bigger sibling's 2,110, and pays 18.1 seconds against 7.8 for the identical score.

Perfect scores from everyone, including on a hard tier built specifically to create headroom: an ambiguous-substring lookup that punishes guessing, a manager-chain walk requiring five chained calls, and a proposed fix that looks right and is not. Zero malformed tool calls in 100 runs.

The honest reading: this harness cannot test the Ornith card's headline claim. The 35B card says it beats Gemma 4-31B and Muse Glimmer 30B "by wide margins on agentic coding". At this difficulty there is no margin, because everything is at ceiling. That is a limit of the eval, not a refutation of the claim. The benchmarks behind it, SWE-bench Pro and Terminal-Bench, are multi-hour agent runs against real repositories.

What does separate the field is cost, again: identical 18/18, and Ornith 35B finishes a task in 4.9 seconds while Muse takes 23.4 and burns 6,192 characters of thinking to Ornith's 2,256.

Card claims

What the model card says, against what the rig measured

Held "~3B activated parameters per token" Consistent with everything measured. 256 experts of width 512, 8 active plus a shared one, and decode speed 3.5× a 27B dense model confirms the activation count behaves as advertised.
Held Context window of 262,144 tokens The only large model in the field that actually delivers it on 32 GB, at full speed, with 5.8 GiB spare. Gemma manages 56% of the same nominal window.
Untested "Outperforms Gemma 4-31B and Muse Glimmer-30B by wide margins on agentic coding" Not reproducible here in either direction. All five models scored 18/18 on this harness, so it lacks the resolution to separate them. Ornith was 3.4× faster per task than Gemma and 4.8× faster than Muse at equal accuracy.
Untested SWE-bench Verified 79, Terminal-Bench 67.8 Out of scope for a single-card local benchmark. These require agent harnesses running for hours against real repositories.
Omitted Nothing about the MTP head Both GGUFs ship a functional nextn block worth up to 1.5× on structured output. Neither card mentions it, and it is off unless you know the flag.
Suspect 9B card recommends presence_penalty 1.5 for general tasks This is the setting that caused runaway thinking on Qwen 3.5 in earlier work on this rig. It appears inherited from the Qwen ancestor. It did not cause failures in this run, but nothing suggests it was re-validated for Ornith.

Ornith-1.5-35B-A3B is the model to run on a 32 GB card. Fastest decode, fastest correct answer, highest throughput under concurrency, and the only large model that holds a real 262k context. It costs 19.7 GiB and it is slow on very short prompts. Those are the two prices.

Ornith-1.5-9B is the efficiency winner at 36.7 tok/s per gibibyte, seven times Gemma's rate, and it holds the full context window in 14.6 GiB. If you want to run something alongside the model, this is the one.

Gemma 4-31B is a poor fit for this hardware. Not because of its quality, which matched everyone at 8/8 and 18/18, but because 80 KiB of KV per token means every axis that adds memory pressure breaks it: a context ceiling at 56% of nominal, negative concurrency scaling past four slots, and 205 seconds to answer a 128k question.

And nothing here separates these five models on quality, because at this task difficulty they are all correct. That may be the most useful finding for anyone choosing among them: pick on speed, memory, and context, because the accuracy difference is not measurable at this scale.

Method and limits

What was measured, and what I got wrong on the way

One runtime for all five models, llama-server build b10448, every model at Q4_K_M so quant class is never a confound, full GPU offload, f16 KV, flash attention on, --jinja with each model's own template. Decode used the raw completion endpoint with ignore_eos so every model emitted exactly 512 tokens and no early stop could inflate a rate. Timings come from the server's own instrumentation. Sampling was each model card's own recommendation, logged per row. Grading is pure functions throughout, with ground truth computed independently from the fixture data; the agentic environment never executes model-written code.

Two corrections made during the run

The context ceiling was reported wrongly twice. The first search stepped up once on success and stopped, so it reported the last passing probe as a ceiling; no probe ever failed for any model. The fixed search then reported all five models holding 262,144, which was also wrong, because on Windows the allocation never fails at all — it spills to system RAM silently. Only the third method, measuring the decode-rate cliff, produces a number that means anything. The two superseded result files are retained in the repository under names marking them invalid.

One agentic task was defective. Task A1 ended "report the timezone of that region", which reads as asking for the timezone alone. All five models answered exactly UTC+1 and a grader demanding two fields marked all five wrong. That is an ambiguous prompt, not a capability difference, so the item is excluded from every score above and the wording is fixed in the source.

Not measured

  • Vision. Both Ornith repositories ship an mmproj file and the templates carry image and video tokens. No multimodal testing was done.
  • Quality cost of KV quantization. The q8_0 and q4_0 sizes are measured; what they do to output is not.
  • Quality cost of MTP. It is established that the output differs; whether it is worse is unknown.
  • Real agentic coding at SWE-bench scale, which is where the card's central claim lives.
  • Reverse-order control runs, dropped deliberately. Run order can manufacture roughly a 4% effect on this power-capped card, so any two models within 5% of each other on decode speed should be read as tied. No conclusion above rests on a gap that small.

Ten phases, five models, roughly 500 measured runs on a single RTX 5090. Raw results as JSONL, the harness, and the protocol fixed before data collection are in the project repository alongside this report.

Superseded and invalidated result files are kept rather than deleted, under filenames that say so.
