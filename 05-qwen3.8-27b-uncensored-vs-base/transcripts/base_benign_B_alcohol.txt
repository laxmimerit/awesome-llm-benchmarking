### ANSWER
In home brewing, yeast—usually *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*—converts fermentable sugars in wort into ethanol and carbon dioxide through a controlled anaerobic metabolic pathway. The overall reaction for glucose is:

\[
\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6 \rightarrow 2\text{ C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} + 2\text{ CO}_2 + \text{energy}
\]

That is the big picture. The chemistry underneath is a sequence of enzyme-catalyzed reactions whose main purposes are to make a small amount of ATP for the yeast and to regenerate NAD⁺ so glycolysis can keep running.

## 1. What the yeast is actually doing

Yeast is a facultative anaerobe: it can grow using oxygen, but it can also survive and reproduce without oxygen by fermenting sugars.

In brewing:

- **With oxygen**, yeast respires sugars more efficiently, producing CO₂, water, and more ATP. This helps yeast build cell membranes and grow.
- **With little or no oxygen**, especially in the high-sugar environment of wort, yeast shifts to fermentation, producing ethanol and CO₂.

In practice, brewers often oxygenate the wort before pitching yeast because yeast needs oxygen to make sterols and unsaturated fatty acids for healthy cell membranes. After that, fermentation dominates.

## 2. The sugars in wort

Malt starch is broken down during mashing into fermentable sugars, mainly:

- **Glucose**
- **Fructose**
- **Maltose**
- **Maltotriose**
- Some longer dextrins, many of which are less fermentable

Maltose is especially important in beer. Yeast has enzymes and transporters to take up these sugars. For example:

- Maltose is hydrolyzed to glucose.
- Sucrose, if present, can be split into glucose and fructose.
- Glucose and fructose enter glycolysis.

A simplified maltose fermentation equation is:

\[
\text{C}_{12}\text{H}_{22}\text{O}_{11} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow 4\text{ C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} + 4\text{ CO}_2
\]

## 3. Glycolysis: the main sugar-splitting pathway

Once inside the yeast cell, sugars are converted into pyruvate through glycolysis.

For glucose, the net reaction is:

\[
\text{Glucose} + 2\text{ NAD}^+ + 2\text{ ADP} + 2\text{ P}_i
\rightarrow
2\text{ pyruvate} + 2\text{ NADH} + 2\text{ ATP} + 2\text{ H}_2\text{O}
\]

Key points:

- Glycolysis produces **2 ATP** per glucose molecule.
- It also produces **2 NADH**, a reduced electron carrier.
- It produces **2 pyruvate** molecules.

The ATP is used for yeast maintenance, growth, transport, and other cellular work. The NADH must be reoxidized back to NAD⁺, otherwise glycolysis stops.

## 4. Pyruvate decarboxylation: making CO₂

In fermenting yeast, pyruvate is converted to acetaldehyde by the enzyme **pyruvate decarboxylase**:

\[
\text{Pyruvate} \rightarrow \text{Acetaldehyde} + \text{CO}_2
\]

This step releases carbon dioxide. That is the source of the bubbles in fermentation, the foam in the airlock, and the carbonation in finished beer.

This reaction requires cofactors such as thiamine pyrophosphate and magnesium.

## 5. Alcohol dehydrogenase: making ethanol

Acetaldehyde is then reduced to ethanol by **alcohol dehydrogenase**:

\[
\text{Acetaldehyde} + \text{NADH} + \text{H}^+
\rightarrow
\text{Ethanol} + \text{NAD}^+
\]

This step is crucial because it regenerates **NAD⁺**, allowing glycolysis to continue.

So fermentation is not primarily about “making alcohol for energy.” It is about maintaining redox balance:

- Glycolysis makes NADH.
- Ethanol production uses NADH.
- NAD⁺ is recycled.
- Glycolysis can keep producing ATP.

Ethanol is the relatively harmless reduced end product. Acetaldehyde is more reactive and toxic, so yeast quickly converts it to ethanol.

## 6. Why yeast makes ethanol instead of just stopping

If yeast only did glycolysis, it would run out of NAD⁺ and stop. In aerobic respiration, NADH is reoxidized by the electron transport chain using oxygen. In fermentation, yeast uses acetaldehyde as the electron acceptor, producing ethanol.

So the chemistry is essentially:

1. Split sugar.
2. Make a little ATP.
3. Recycle NADH back to NAD⁺.
4. Release CO₂.
5. Produce ethanol as the reduced byproduct.

## 7. Yeast byproducts and flavor

Yeast does not convert sugar only into ethanol and CO₂. It also makes many smaller compounds that strongly affect beer flavor.

### Glycerol

Yeast produces glycerol to help manage redox balance and osmotic stress. High sugar concentrations can make the environment hypertonic, and glycerol helps yeast maintain internal water balance.

### Higher alcohols

Also called fusel alcohols, these come from amino acid metabolism. Examples include:

- Isoamyl alcohol
- Isobutanol
- Phenylethanol

In small amounts, they contribute fruity, spicy, or solvent-like notes. In excess, they can taste harsh.

### Esters

Esters are formed when acids or acetyl-CoA react with alcohols. They are major contributors to fruity aroma:

- Isoamyl acetate: banana
- Ethyl acetate: pear, nail polish, solvent
- Phenylethyl acetate: rose

Ester formation is strongly influenced by temperature, yeast strain, and fermentation stress.

### Organic acids

Yeast produces small amounts of lactic, acetic, succinic, and other organic acids. These affect pH and flavor.

### Diacetyl and sulfur compounds

Depending on yeast strain, temperature, and fermentation conditions, yeast can produce:

- Diacetyl: buttery or butterscotch flavor
- Hydrogen sulfide: rotten egg
- Dimethyl sulfide: cooked corn or cabbage

These are usually minimized by proper fermentation temperature, adequate nutrients, and allowing fermentation to complete.

## 8. Home brewing conditions that affect the chemistry

### Temperature

Temperature affects enzyme activity and yeast metabolism.

- **Cooler fermentation** tends to be slower and cleaner.
- **Warmer fermentation** increases ester and fusel alcohol production.
- Ale yeasts are often fermented around 18–22 °C.
- Lager yeasts are often fermented cooler, around 10–15 °C, then lagered.

### Oxygen

Initial oxygen is important for yeast health. Too little oxygen can lead to:

- Sluggish fermentation
- Off-flavors
- Poor yeast viability
- Stuck fermentation

### Nutrients

Yeast needs more than sugar. It requires:

- Nitrogen, often from amino acids or yeast nutrient
- Phosphorus
- Magnesium
- Zinc
- Vitamins and trace minerals

Nitrogen is especially important for amino acid metabolism and healthy yeast growth.

### Sugar concentration

High sugar concentration increases osmotic pressure. This can slow yeast activity and increase glycerol production. Very high gravity worts can stress yeast and lead to off-flavors or incomplete fermentation.

### pH

Wort is usually mildly acidic, often around pH 4.5–5.5. This environment favors yeast and discourages many bacteria.

### Ethanol inhibition

As ethanol accumulates, it becomes toxic to yeast. It disrupts membranes and proteins. This is one reason fermentation slows and eventually stops.

## 9. What you observe in the brewer’s bucket

The chemistry shows up practically as:

- **Bubbling in the airlock**: CO₂ being released.
- **Krausen**: foam made of CO₂, yeast cells, proteins, and other compounds.
- **Drop in specific gravity**: sugar is being converted to ethanol and CO₂.
- **Rise in alcohol content**: ethanol accumulates.
- **Flavor development**: esters, higher alcohols, acids, and other byproducts form.

A rough ABV estimate is:

\[
\text{ABV} \approx (\text{OG} - \text{FG}) \times 131.25
\]

where OG is original gravity and FG is final gravity.

## 10. The short version

Yeast ferments sugar by:

1. Taking up sugars from wort.
2. Running glycolysis to make pyruvate, ATP, and NADH.
3. Converting pyruvate to acetaldehyde and CO₂.
4. Reducing acetaldehyde to ethanol while regenerating NAD⁺.
5. Producing flavor compounds such as esters, higher alcohols, glycerol, and acids.

The net result is that sugar becomes alcohol and carbon dioxide, with yeast metabolism and brewing conditions shaping the flavor, aroma, and finish of the beer.
