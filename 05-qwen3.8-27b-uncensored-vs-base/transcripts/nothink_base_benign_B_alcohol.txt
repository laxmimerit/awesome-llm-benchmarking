### ANSWER
In home brewing, yeast converts sugars into ethanol and carbon dioxide through **alcoholic fermentation**. The main sugar sources are usually glucose, maltose, and other fermentable sugars from malted grains, fruit, honey, or added sugar.

The overall reaction is:

**C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2 C₂H₅OH + 2 CO₂ + energy**

That means one molecule of glucose becomes two molecules of ethanol and two molecules of carbon dioxide.

## 1. Yeast takes sugar into the cell

Yeast cells, such as *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*, absorb sugars from the wort or must. Inside the cell, the sugar enters a metabolic pathway called **glycolysis**.

Glycolysis breaks a six-carbon sugar molecule into two three-carbon molecules called **pyruvate**.

Simplified:

**Glucose → 2 pyruvate + 2 ATP + 2 NADH**

This step produces a small amount of usable energy, ATP, and reduces NAD⁺ to NADH.

## 2. Pyruvate is converted to acetaldehyde

Under low-oxygen conditions, which are typical in brewing, yeast does not send pyruvate into the mitochondria for full aerobic respiration. Instead, it converts pyruvate into **acetaldehyde**.

This reaction is catalyzed by the enzyme **pyruvate decarboxylase**:

**Pyruvate → acetaldehyde + CO₂**

This is where the carbon dioxide bubbles in brewing come from.

## 3. Acetaldehyde is reduced to ethanol

The acetaldehyde is then reduced to ethanol by the enzyme **alcohol dehydrogenase**.

**Acetaldehyde + NADH → ethanol + NAD⁺**

This step is crucial because it regenerates **NAD⁺**, which is needed for glycolysis to continue. Without NAD⁺, the cell would run out of electron carriers and sugar breakdown would stop.

So fermentation is not primarily about making alcohol for energy; it is about **regenerating NAD⁺** so yeast can keep producing ATP from sugar under anaerobic conditions.

## 4. Why yeast makes ethanol

Yeast can produce much more energy by respiring aerobically, using oxygen to fully oxidize sugar into carbon dioxide and water. But in brewing, oxygen is limited after the initial yeast growth phase.

Without oxygen, yeast switches to fermentation. Ethanol is a waste product that yeast can excrete, allowing the cell to keep making ATP from sugar.

## 5. What happens in a home brew

In a home brewery, the process usually looks like this:

1. **Mashing or sugar extraction**  
   Starches from grain are converted into fermentable sugars, or sugars are already present in fruit, honey, or other sources.

2. **Yeast is added**  
   Yeast cells begin consuming oxygen and nutrients, growing and reproducing.

3. **Fermentation begins**  
   As oxygen becomes scarce, yeast shifts to anaerobic fermentation.

4. **Sugar becomes ethanol and CO₂**  
   The liquid becomes alcoholic, and CO₂ is released. In a sealed vessel, pressure builds; in a carboy with an airlock, CO₂ escapes while oxygen is kept out.

5. **Fermentation slows and stops**  
   Fermentation stops when fermentable sugars are depleted, ethanol concentration becomes too high, temperature is unsuitable, or nutrients become limiting.

## 6. Byproducts

Yeast does not only make ethanol and CO₂. It also produces smaller amounts of:

- **Glycerol**
- **Higher alcohols**, such as isoamyl alcohol
- **Esters**, which contribute fruity aromas
- **Acids**, such as acetic and lactic acid in small amounts
- **Sulfur compounds**, which can smell like rotten eggs or struck matches if produced in excess

These byproducts are important in beer, wine, and cider because they affect flavor and aroma.

## 7. Factors that affect fermentation

Several chemical and physical factors influence how well yeast ferments:

- **Temperature**  
  Too cold slows yeast activity. Too hot can stress yeast and create off-flavors.

- **Oxygen**  
  Yeast needs some oxygen early for cell membrane synthesis, but too much oxygen later can reduce fermentation efficiency.

- **pH**  
  Yeast generally prefers mildly acidic conditions.

- **Nutrients**  
  Yeast needs nitrogen, vitamins, and minerals to grow and ferment properly.

- **Sugar concentration**  
  Very high sugar levels can create osmotic stress, slowing yeast activity.

- **Ethanol concentration**  
  Ethanol becomes toxic to yeast at higher levels, usually limiting fermentation to around 10–15% ABV for many brewing strains, though some can tolerate more.

## In short

Yeast ferments sugar by breaking it down through glycolysis, converting pyruvate to acetaldehyde, and then reducing acetaldehyde to ethanol. This regenerates NAD⁺, allowing the yeast to keep producing energy from sugar without oxygen. The result is ethanol, carbon dioxide, heat, and flavor compounds that give home-brewed beer, wine, or cider its character.
