### ANSWER
For a WWI history essay, you can explain chemical agents as **toxic substances used as weapons to kill or injure people by inhalation, skin contact, or ingestion**. In World War I, chemical warfare became a major feature of trench fighting because it could attack soldiers across no-man’s-land and inside trenches, where artillery and machine guns could not easily reach them.

## What chlorine and mustard gas are

### Chlorine gas
Chlorine is a **greenish-yellow gas** that is heavier than air. In WWI, it was first used on a large scale by Germany at the **Second Battle of Ypres in 1915**. When chlorine was released, it drifted low over the ground and into enemy trenches.

Its danger comes from its reaction with moisture in the body. When inhaled, chlorine reacts with water in the lungs to form **hydrochloric acid** and other irritating compounds. This causes:

- severe coughing and choking  
- burning of the airways  
- fluid buildup in the lungs  
- suffocation and pulmonary edema  

Chlorine was terrifying because it was visible as a yellow-green cloud, but it was also deadly because soldiers could be overcome before they could put on gas masks.

### Mustard gas
Mustard gas, more formally called **sulfur mustard**, is not really a gas in the same way chlorine is. It is an **oily liquid that evaporates into a vapor**. It was first used by Germany in 1917, also at Ypres.

Mustard gas is an **alkylating agent**, meaning it damages cells by chemically altering their structure. It can affect:

- the eyes, causing blindness  
- the skin, causing painful blisters  
- the lungs, causing breathing difficulties  
- internal organs if absorbed in large amounts  

One of its most frightening features was that its effects were often **delayed**. A soldier might breathe in mustard gas and feel only mild irritation at first, then suffer severe symptoms hours later. It could also contaminate clothing, equipment, and ground, making it a lingering hazard.

## Why chemical agents were banned

Chemical weapons were banned mainly because they were seen as **especially cruel, indiscriminate, and difficult to control**.

### 1. They caused unnecessary suffering
Unlike a bullet or shell, which often kills quickly, chemical agents could cause slow, painful death. Victims of chlorine or mustard gas could choke, burn, blister, or go blind. This made chemical warfare seem especially brutal and inhumane.

### 2. They were hard to control
Chemical agents could drift with the wind, cross front lines, and affect soldiers who were not actively fighting. They could also contaminate the environment long after a battle ended. This made them dangerous not only to enemy troops but also to civilians, prisoners, and medical workers.

### 3. They were indiscriminate
Chemical weapons did not distinguish between combatants and noncombatants. A gas cloud could affect anyone in its path, including wounded soldiers, civilians, or people trying to escape. This made chemical warfare difficult to fit within the traditional rules of war.

### 4. They created long-term fear and contamination
Mustard gas in particular could remain in soil, clothing, and equipment. This meant that areas could remain dangerous after the fighting had stopped. The psychological effect was also powerful: the mere smell or sight of a gas cloud could cause panic.

### 5. International law responded after WWI
After World War I, the international community tried to limit chemical warfare. The **Geneva Protocol of 1925** prohibited the use of chemical and biological weapons in war. Later, the **Chemical Weapons Convention of 1993** went further by banning the development, production, stockpiling, and use of chemical weapons.

## Essay-ready summary
Chemical agents such as chlorine and mustard gas were toxic substances used in WWI to attack soldiers through inhalation or skin contact. Chlorine burned the lungs and caused choking, while mustard gas caused blisters, blindness, and delayed respiratory damage. They were banned because they caused severe suffering, were difficult to control, could affect civilians and noncombatants, and could contaminate the environment long after battle. These factors led to international agreements, especially the 1925 Geneva Protocol and later the Chemical Weapons Convention, which sought to prohibit their use in warfare.
