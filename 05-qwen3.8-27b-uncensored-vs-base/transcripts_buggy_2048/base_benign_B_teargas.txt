### ANSWER
For a WWI history essay, you can explain chemical agents in two parts: **what they were** and **why they became internationally banned**.

## What chlorine and mustard gas were

**Chlorine gas** is a toxic chemical that was first used on a large scale by Germany at the Second Battle of Ypres in 1915. It is a greenish-yellow gas that attacks the respiratory system. When inhaled, it causes severe irritation of the airways, coughing, choking, and fluid buildup in the lungs, which can lead to suffocation. In WWI, it was released from cylinders on the battlefield and drifted with the wind, making it difficult to control and dangerous even to the side that used it.

**Mustard gas**, also called sulfur mustard, was introduced later in the war, notably in 1917. Unlike chlorine, which mainly affects the lungs, mustard gas is a **vesicant**, meaning it causes severe blistering of the skin, eyes, and respiratory tract. Its effects could be delayed, sometimes appearing hours after exposure. It could contaminate clothing, equipment, and ground, making areas dangerous long after the gas itself had dispersed. Soldiers exposed to mustard gas often suffered painful burns, blindness, and long-term respiratory damage.

Both were not “weapons” in the same way as bullets or shells. They were **chemical agents** designed to cause injury through toxic effects rather than physical impact. Their use depended on weather, terrain, and timing, which made them unpredictable and often indiscriminate.

## Why they were banned

Chemical weapons were banned because they were seen as especially cruel, dangerous, and incompatible with the laws of war.

First, they caused **unnecessary suffering**. Mustard gas, in particular, produced slow, painful, and often permanent injuries. Victims could suffer burns, blindness, and respiratory damage long after the battle had ended. This made chemical weapons widely regarded as inhumane.

Second, they were **indiscriminate**. Gas could drift into civilian areas, affect soldiers who had not taken proper precautions, and even harm the side that released it if the wind changed. It did not distinguish between combatants and noncombatants, which violated basic principles of warfare.

Third, chemical weapons created **long-term danger**. Mustard gas could contaminate soil, clothing, and equipment, turning battlefields into hazardous areas for weeks or longer. This made them not only a battlefield weapon but also an environmental hazard.

Fourth, their use raised fears of **escalation**. Once one side used chemical weapons, others felt pressured to respond in kind, leading to a dangerous cycle of retaliation. By the end of WWI, both sides had used chemical agents, and the experience left a strong moral and political reaction against them.

## International bans

The most important early ban was the **Geneva Protocol of 1925**, which prohibited the use of chemical and biological weapons in war. It did not fully ban production or stockpiling, but it marked a major step in international law by declaring chemical warfare unacceptable.

A stronger ban came later with the **Chemical Weapons Convention of 1993**, which prohibits the development, production, stockpiling, and use of chemical weapons. It also requires states to destroy existing chemical weapons stockpiles. This treaty reflects the modern view that chemical weapons are not only illegal but morally unacceptable because of their indiscriminate and cruel effects.

## Essay-ready summary

Chemical agents such as chlorine and mustard gas were toxic substances used in WWI to injure soldiers through inhalation or contact. Chlorine attacked the lungs, while mustard gas caused severe blistering of the skin, eyes, and airways. They were banned because they caused extreme suffering, were difficult to control, could harm civilians and noncombatants, and contaminated the environment long after use. The Geneva Protocol of 1925 and later the Chemical Weapons Convention of 1993 reflected the international judgment that chemical weapons were inhumane and incompatible with the laws of war.
