### ANSWER
In home brewing, yeast—usually *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*—converts fermentable sugars in wort into ethanol and carbon dioxide. The key chemistry is **alcoholic fermentation**, a metabolic pathway that lets yeast make a small amount of ATP without oxygen.

## Overall reaction

For glucose:

\[
\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6 \rightarrow 2\ \text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} + 2\ \text{CO}_2
\]

So one molecule of glucose becomes two molecules of ethanol and two molecules of carbon dioxide. In a homebrew fermenter, the CO₂ is what you see as bubbles in the airlock and what can carbonate beer if trapped in a sealed bottle.

## Why yeast makes alcohol

Yeast needs to regenerate **NAD⁺** so glycolysis can keep running.

During glycolysis, glucose is broken down to pyruvate, producing:

- 2 ATP net
- 2 NADH
- 2 pyruvate

If NADH is not converted back to NAD⁺, glycolysis stops. Under low-oxygen, high-sugar brewing conditions, yeast regenerates NAD⁺ by reducing pyruvate-derived acetaldehyde to ethanol.

## Step-by-step chemistry

### 1. Sugar uptake and conversion

Wort contains glucose, maltose, maltotriose, and other sugars. Yeast transports sugars into the cell. Maltose is hydrolyzed by maltase into glucose:

\[
\text{maltose} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow 2\ \text{glucose}
\]

### 2. Glycolysis

Glucose is converted to pyruvate in the cytoplasm:

\[
\text{glucose} + 2\ \text{NAD}^+ + 2\ \text{ADP} + 2\ P_i
\rightarrow
2\ \text{pyruvate} + 2\ \text{NADH} + 2\ \text{ATP} + 2\ \text{H}_2\text{O}
\]

This is the main energy-producing step.

### 3. Pyruvate decarboxylation

Pyruvate loses CO₂ to form acetaldehyde:

\[
\text{pyruvate} \rightarrow \text{acetaldehyde} + \text{CO}_2
\]

This step is catalyzed by **pyruvate decarboxylase**, which requires thiamine pyrophosphate, a form of vitamin B1.

### 4. Ethanol formation

Acetaldehyde is reduced to ethanol using NADH:

\[
\text{acetaldehyde} + \text{NADH} + \text{H}^+
\rightarrow
\text{ethanol} + \text{NAD}^+
\]

This regenerates NAD⁺, allowing glycolysis to continue.

## What happens in a homebrew fermenter

### CO₂ production

The CO₂ from fermentation creates pressure and bubbles. In an open or airlocked fermenter, it escapes. In a sealed bottle, it dissolves into the beer and creates carbonation.

### Ethanol accumulation

Ethanol is toxic to yeast at high concentrations. Most ale yeasts stop fermenting or become stressed around 5–12% ABV, depending on strain and conditions. High alcohol disrupts cell membranes and enzyme function.

### Heat production

Fermentation is exothermic. Yeast metabolism releases heat, which can raise the fermenter temperature and affect flavor.

### Yeast health and byproducts

Yeast also makes flavor compounds:

- **Esters**: fruity aromas
- **Higher alcohols/fusel alcohols**: spicy or solvent-like notes
- **Acetaldehyde**: green-apple off-flavor if fermentation is rushed
- **Diacetyl**: buttery note if yeast is stressed or fermentation is interrupted
- **Sulfur compounds**: rotten-egg or cabbage notes under certain stress conditions

These are produced in small amounts and depend heavily on yeast strain, temperature, oxygen, nutrients, and fermentation speed.

## Oxygen’s role

Yeast do not need oxygen to ferment sugar, but they do need some oxygen early in brewing. Oxygen is used to make **ergosterol** and unsaturated fatty acids, which are essential for healthy yeast cell membranes. Without enough initial oxygen, yeast can ferment poorly, stall, or produce off-flavors.

## Practical chemistry implications for home brewing

- **Temperature control** affects enzyme activity and byproduct formation.
- **Nutrients** such as nitrogen, phosphorus, magnesium, zinc, and B vitamins support yeast metabolism.
- **Oxygenation** before pitching helps yeast build healthy cells.
